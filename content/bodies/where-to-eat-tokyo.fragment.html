Where to Eat in Tokyo: A First-Timer's Neighbourhood Guide

Breakfast at Tsukiji, snacks under the tracks at Ameyoko, yakitori in Shinjuku's memory lane: Tokyo, one neighbourhood at a time.

Researched guide: built from public guides and reference sources, not a first-hand visit. How we research.

Tokyo is the easiest big city in the world to eat well in and the hardest to choose in. Wikipedia's ramen entry alone puts the city at around 5,000 ramen shops, and that's before sushi counters, yakitori alleys and basement food halls. The trick for a first visit isn't finding a good meal; it's picking a few neighbourhoods and letting each one do what it does best.

1. Tsukiji Outer Market: breakfast seafood and kitchen shops

The famous wholesale fish market moved to Toyosu in October 2018, but the outer market in Tsukiji, between the Sumida River and Ginza, stayed put. It's a tangle of retail stalls, sushi restaurants, grocers and knife and kitchen-tool shops. Go early: historically most outer-market shops wound down by early afternoon, so treat it as a breakfast-and-brunch neighbourhood. Graze on grilled seafood, tamagoyaki (sweet rolled omelette) and a sushi set, then browse the kitchenware.

2. Ueno and Ameyoko: a street market by the tracks

Ameya-Yokochō, or Ameyoko, runs alongside the JR Yamanote Line from Ueno Station towards Okachimachi, with more than 250 shops selling everything from dried fish and candy to clothes. Its name may come from the candy shops (ame-ya) of the post-war years. It's a stand-up, snack-as-you-go neighbourhood: fruit on sticks, seafood stands and cheap counters under the railway arches.

3. Shinjuku: memory lane and a basement food hall

Northwest of Shinjuku Station, Omoide Yokocho ("memory lane") is a small network of alleys along the tracks packed with tiny eateries serving yakitori, kushiyaki, ramen and soba. Many are just a counter and a few stools, and most open from around 5 pm. For the daytime version of Tokyo food culture, head to a depachika, the department-store food basement: Isetan's Shinjuku flagship, Takashimaya and Keio all have food departments below ground. Think bento, wagashi (Japanese sweets), pickles and immaculate fruit.

4. Ramen neighbourhoods: Ikebukuro, Ogikubo and Ebisu

Tokyo-style ramen is a soy-seasoned chicken broth with slightly thin, curly noodles, often with a touch of dashi. Ikebukuro, Ogikubo and Ebisu are often named as Tokyo's ramen areas. Most counters use a ticket machine: you pay first, hand over the ticket and sit down. New to the styles? Read our Ramen 101 before you go.

5. Asakusa: where Tokyo ramen began

Ramen historians trace the first specialised ramen shop, Rairaiken, to Asakusa in 1910. The original closed in 1976, and Wikipedia reports a descendant of the founder reopened it in Asakusa in 2026, which makes Asakusa a fitting stop for noodle history fans. Pair it with the old-Tokyo snack stalls around the temple district.

6. Konbini: the 24-hour safety net

Japan's convenience stores (7-Eleven, Lawson, FamilyMart) are a food stop in their own right: onigiri, fried chicken, and the much-photographed egg sando. They're perfect for a cheap breakfast or a late-night bite on the way back to your hotel.

Good to know

  • Pay first: ticket machines are common at ramen and casual counters.
  • Small places, small groups: alley bars and counters often seat fewer than ten people.
  • Timing: markets are for mornings; yokocho alleys come alive after dark.
  • Watch for touts in Kabukicho: japan-guide warns about exorbitant cover charges at tout-run bars.
